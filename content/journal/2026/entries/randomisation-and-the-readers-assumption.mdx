## The shorthand and the reality

"Randomised" functions in our reading as a word of approval. It appears to
summarise the design, implying that the study's conclusions are correspondingly
safe. This is a reasonable instinct and a dangerous one, because randomisation
guarantees some things and not others.

What allocation randomisation does provide is *balance in expectation*. Groups
should be comparable on measured and unmeasured characteristics, so that
differences in outcome cannot plausibly be attributed to anything except the
intervention. This is a strong and genuinely valuable property.

What it does not provide is protection against problems that arise after
allocation. It does not prevent differential adherence, in which one group
takes the intervention more consistently than the other. It does not blind
outcome assessment. It does not compensate for an intervention that is
inadequate, a control group that is contaminated, or a measurement applied
differently by group. It says nothing about a subgroup defined after the fact.

## The most common error

The error we encounter most often in journal club is the extension of
randomisation's protection to an unrandomised comparison within a randomised
trial.

A trial may randomise treatment assignment and then compare subgroups defined
by a post-randomisation characteristic — baseline severity, biomarker status,
a threshold on a continuous variable. That comparison is observational. The
balance randomisation provides does not extend to it, because the characteristic
defining the subgroup was not randomised.

The template is always the same: "In this randomised trial of 2,000 patients,
those with high baseline risk had substantially higher mortality." That
statement may be true and entirely uninformative, because high baseline risk
confers higher mortality regardless of treatment.

## Why the error persists

Randomisation is taught early and labelled once. Its name becomes a proxy for
rigour that is carried forward without re-derivation, and it is reinforced
because randomised studies are more likely to be cited, funded and repeated.

The practical defence is to ask, of any comparison in a paper: *what was
randomised?* If the answer is "nothing, in this comparison", the comparison is
observational regardless of the study's title.

## Closing

Randomisation remains the most important methodological development in the
history of clinical research. The point is not to qualify it but to use it
precisely — to ask what it secured, and to notice the claims it was never asked
to secure.
