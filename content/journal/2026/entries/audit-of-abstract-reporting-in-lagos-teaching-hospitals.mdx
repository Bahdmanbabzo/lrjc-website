## Background

The quality of a research literature is bounded by the quality of its
reporting. International guidelines — CONSORT for trials, STROBE for
observational studies — specify what must be reported for a reader to judge
whether a study's conclusions are warranted. Adoption of these guidelines in
Nigerian medical journals has not, to our knowledge, been formally assessed.

## Methods

We identified all original research articles published between 2020 and 2024 in
four peer-reviewed medical journals indexed in the Nigerian Journals Online
database. Abstracts were extracted and independently assessed by two reviewers
against the applicable CONSORT or STROBE checklist. Disagreements were resolved
by consensus.

The primary outcome was the proportion of applicable reporting items satisfied.
We did not assess the underlying methodological quality of any study, only the
completeness of its reporting.

## Results

We assessed 412 abstracts: 148 randomised or quasi-experimental studies and 264
observational studies. The median number of satisfied items was 9 of 17
(randomised) and 11 of 22 (observational).

The most frequently satisfied items were those describing the setting and the
participants. The least satisfied were justification of sample size (14%),
reporting of missing data (11%), and declaration of funding and conflicts of
interest (18%).

Reporting completeness did not improve across the five-year period
(p > 0.05 for trend).

## Discussion

The pattern we observed — a descriptive abstract that reads well and an
analytical one that is largely absent — is consistent with a literature
optimised for clinical usefulness rather than methodological transparency.

We make no claim about the underlying quality of the studies assessed. A study
may be methodologically sound and reported poorly, or the reverse. The finding
of this audit concerns the report, not the research.

### Limitations

We assessed abstracts only, not full texts, because full texts were unavailable
for a substantial proportion of articles. We excluded journals without
online indexing. Checklist adherence is a proxy for transparency, not a
measure of scientific validity, and we caution against reading our percentages
as quality scores.

## Conclusion

Substantial gaps exist in the reporting of studies published in the journals
audited, particularly for the items that allow a reader to judge bias. Journal
editorial boards, training programmes and institutional research offices all have
a role in closing this gap, and the reporting guidelines themselves are
inexpensive to adopt.
