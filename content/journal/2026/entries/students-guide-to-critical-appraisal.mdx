## Before you read the abstract

The abstract is written to be read without the paper. That is precisely why it
is the most dangerous place to stop.

Read the title and the methods section first. If the methods do not match the
claim, no amount of persuasive writing in the results will change what the
study shows.

## Six questions, in order

**1. What was the question, and was it answerable?**
Vague questions produce vague conclusions. "Is metformin effective?" is not a
research question; "does metformin reduce HbA1c in treatment-naive adults with
type 2 diabetes over 12 months?" is.

**2. Who was actually studied, and who is the conclusion about?**
Read the inclusion criteria and the flow of participants. A study that enrolled
2,000 people but analysed 400, or analysed a group far younger or healthier than
the patients you see, supports a narrower claim than its abstract suggests.

**3. What is being compared with what?**
The comparator determines whether a result matters. A new test compared against
an obsolete standard, or a new dose against a placebo rather than against
current practice, tells you less than it appears to.

**4. What was measured, and how could that measurement mislead?**
Surrogate outcomes — HbA1c, blood pressure, tumour size — are not the outcomes
patients care about, and they can improve while outcomes worsen. Ask what
changed for the person.

**5. What happened to the participants who left?**
Look for the flow diagram. Attrition concentrated in one group, and analyses
restricted to those who completed follow-up, can manufacture a result.

**6. Does the size of the effect matter clinically?
A statistically significant reduction in a very large population may matter less
to an individual patient than a non-significant one in a small population. Only
one of those is reported in the abstract.

## Two habits worth forming early

**Write the conclusion first.** Before reading the discussion, state what the
study actually establishes, in one sentence. Then read the authors' conclusion
and note the difference. The gap between those two sentences is where critical
appraisal lives.

**Separate the claim from the field's use of it.** A paper's own hedging is
usually accurate. The claim that reaches you two years later is usually larger.
It helps to know which one you read.

## On reading something that changes your mind

It is uncomfortable to revise a belief you have held, and it will happen. The
standard to apply is whether the new evidence is a better account of the same
facts — not whether it is more surprising, and not whether it is more
convenient.
