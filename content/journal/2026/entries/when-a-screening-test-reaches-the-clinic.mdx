## Why accuracy is the wrong threshold

A screening test is usually evaluated on sensitivity and specificity, and both
matter. Neither is sufficient.

Screening programmes are judged on something else: whether the population's
health improves. A test with excellent accuracy that arrives at a facility
already unable to treat what it detects, or that displaces capacity from
higher-yield services, may deliver no benefit while consuming substantial
resources.

## Spectrum bias in practice

Diagnostic accuracy studies are usually performed on patients who were already
referred, because that is who is available in hospital. The disease spectrum of
a referred population is not the spectrum of a community being screened — often
far more advanced, and enriched for atypical presentations.

The result is a test that performs well in the literature and underperforms in
the field. Where local validation has been done, performance has frequently
been worse than the original report, and the difference is usually attributable
to spectrum, image quality, or operator performance rather than to biology.

## Capacity displacement

Introducing screening changes the allocation of attention. Appointments,
laboratory capacity, and clinician time are finite. Screening identifies
individuals who may need follow-up; the same resources withdrawn from
symptomatic care will be felt by patients who are already unwell.

This is the least discussed and most consequential effect in most screening
evaluations, and it is systematically absent from the trials that support
adoption. Programmes are usually evaluated on the individuals they detect, not
on the individuals whose care was displaced.

## Pre-adoption checklist

We propose that any screening test considered for local adoption answer the
following in writing, before introduction:

1. **Local validation** — has the test been evaluated in a population
   resembling the target population, and what was the accuracy?
2. **Referral capacity** — what proportion of positive results can be acted on
   within a clinically acceptable interval?
3. **Programme reach** — what proportion of the eligible population will
   actually be screened, given existing attendance patterns?
4. **Displacement** — what service capacity is withdrawn to fund this, and
   what is the expected loss?
5. **Net effect** — what is the expected change in outcomes, taking all five
   answers together rather than detection alone?

A programme that cannot answer the fifth question should not proceed on the
strength of the first.

## Conclusion

Adoption decisions are usually framed as questions of technical capacity. They
are questions of pathway design, and the evidence base for those questions is
thinner than the evidence base for accuracy.
