## Introduction

The overwhelming majority of published clinical research reports a positive
result. This is not because most interventions work. It is partly because null
results are less likely to be written up, less likely to be accepted, and much
less likely to be read.

That asymmetry has consequences, and they are not abstract. A field that
underweights negative evidence will overstate what is known, recommend
interventions that do not help, and commit resources on the strength of a
finding that a larger study later erased.

## Three null results that mattered

**Saturated fat and coronary disease.** The Minnesota Coronary Survey
randomised over 2,200 men to vegetable oil or butter and found no reduction in
coronary death. For five decades this was largely ignored, partly because the
result was inconvenient. Re-analysed in 2016, it became one of the most cited
papers in nutrition — not because it proved anything, but because it constrained
what could be claimed.

**Hormone replacement therapy.** A large randomised trial of hormone therapy in
postmenopausal women found an increase in cardiovascular events and a reduction
in colorectal cancer and hip fracture, and it was stopped early. The dominant
prior belief was reversed. The earlier observational data had looked reassuring
precisely because the women who chose hormone therapy were, on average,
healthier — a confounder that randomisation removed.

**Aspirin for primary prevention.** Benefit in primary prevention remains
genuinely uncertain across risk strata, a conclusion reached largely by
accumulating trials that were individually underpowered. The clearest statements
of the uncertainty come from the null and near-null arms of the literature.

## Why null results are under-read

Three mechanisms compound. Journals publish positive findings because positive
findings interest readers. Authors under-report nulls in submitted manuscripts.
And when nulls are published, teaching tends to skip them because there is no
mechanism story to tell.

The result is a reader who forms an accurate picture of a field's *successful*
investigations and an absent picture of its failed ones — which is precisely
the wrong shape of knowledge for making decisions under uncertainty.

## What readers can do

1. **Read the limitations section of the null studies you cite.** It is where
   the honest boundary of the finding lives.
2. **Prefer reviews that incorporate unpublished nulls.** Their estimates are
   more conservative and usually more accurate.
3. **Ask what the study was powered to detect.** A null result in an
   underpowered trial is not evidence of no effect.
4. **Notice when a claim rests only on positive studies.** That is a finding
   about the literature, not about the intervention.

## Conclusion

Null results are not the absence of knowledge. They are the boundary of it, and
a field that hides its boundaries will eventually be surprised by them.
