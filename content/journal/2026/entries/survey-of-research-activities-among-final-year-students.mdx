## Background

Little is known locally about how many medical students conduct research, what
prevents those who want to from doing so, and whether exposure during
undergraduate years predicts later involvement. We conducted a cross-sectional
survey of final-year students to describe these patterns.

## Methods

All 260 final-year students across three clinical years were invited to
participate via an anonymous online questionnaire. The instrument collected
self-reported research activity, prior exposure to research teaching,
perceived barriers, and interest in postgraduate research.

No attempt was made to verify self-reported outputs, and this limitation is
central to interpreting the results.

## Results

There were 214 responses (82%). Eighty-one participants (38%) had completed at
least one research project, and 29 (14%) had presented externally. Complete
multimodal research exposure during the undergraduate years was reported by 96
(45%).

The most frequently cited barriers were supervisor availability (152 responses),
funding (131), and time compatible with clinical duties (118). A majority of
participants who had no research output (66%) reported wanting one.

Research participation was associated with prior research exposure and with
attendance at research-focused student organisations. It was not associated
with examination performance.

## Discussion

The clearest finding is that interest substantially exceeds opportunity. Most
students who had not done research wanted to, and cited structural barriers
rather than lack of interest.

The association with prior exposure — rather than with academic performance —
suggests that research participation is substantially a function of having been
given a route into it. This is a modifiable factor, and it is the argument for
structured research exposure in the early years rather than only in the final
year.

### Limitations

Self-report throughout; no verification of stated outputs, and we expect
overstatement. Single institution and a single point in time, so the results
describe one cohort's final year and not a trajectory. Recall bias in
estimating past exposure. Response bias is likely given the topic, and we cannot
estimate its direction.

## Conclusion

Research participation among final-year students is limited by structural
barriers rather than by student interest. Early, structured exposure appears
associated with later participation, and would be a reasonable target for
intervention.
