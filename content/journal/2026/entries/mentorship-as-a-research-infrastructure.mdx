## Mentorship as infrastructure

Mentorship is usually described in relational terms: a senior person gives
guidance, a junior person gains it. That description is true and it hides the
operational problem. If research output depends on individuals finding each
other, then capacity is limited by individual availability, and it fails
silently when those individuals leave, become ill, or are overwhelmed by
clinical duties.

A student who never finds a supervisor is not failing through any visible
process. Nothing is reported, because there was no process to report.

## The minimum specification

A research environment produces student work reliably if four things are true.
None requires substantial funding.

**Named supervisors exist for every student who wants to do research.** Not
optional, not self-selected without support, and not dependent on a student's
initiative to negotiate. The absence of a named person is a reportable event.

**Time is allocated rather than donated.** Supervision that depends on a
supervisor's goodwill will be the first thing displaced when the clinic gets
busy, and it will be displaced without anyone deciding to stop.

**Project scope is bounded at the outset.** Students with two years and no
funding cannot run a prospective cohort study. Setting a feasible question
early prevents most of the attrition that occurs between registration and
analysis.

**A public record exists.** A shared list of projects, supervisors and status
serves two purposes at once: it prevents duplicate effort, and it makes absence
visible.

## What mentorship cannot fix

Supervision does not create research funding, laboratory access, ethics
turnaround, or statistical support. In settings where these are absent, a
well-supervised student may still be unable to complete a project.

This should be stated plainly to students at the outset, because expectations
that cannot be met produce disengagement that is then read as a lack of
interest. The honest framing is that supervision determines whether a project
is done well, and infrastructure determines whether it can be done at all.

## Measuring whether it works

The useful measures are not counts of supervised projects. They are the rate at
which started projects reach a written output, the time from proposal to
first result, and the proportion of students who report that they could name
who supervised them. Each of these can be collected without a research
budget, and each reveals failure that a project count conceals.

## Conclusion

Research capacity is usually discussed in terms of individuals. Treating
mentorship as a specified, monitored service with named responsibilities would
make the same capacity more reliable, more equitable between students, and
much easier to sustain across staff changes.
