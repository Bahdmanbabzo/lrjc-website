## The optimisation target

A model trained and evaluated on a public benchmark optimises one measurable
quantity on one distribution of data. This is a reasonable thing to do, and it
is what the field rewards. The difficulty is that neither the quantity nor the
distribution corresponds to the clinical question.

## Volume is not location

Dice similarity and similar overlap metrics weight a voxelised volume. A thin
band of error along a tumour boundary contributes almost nothing to either the
intersection or the union, so it is barely penalised.

That is acceptable for volumetric tasks and inadequate for tasks where the
boundary is the decision. In neurosurgical planning, in radiotherapy field
planning, in determining whether a lesion is operable, the boundary is the
entire question. A model can lead a benchmark and be clinically unreliable, and
these two facts are not in tension.

Surface-distance metrics, which penalise boundary error directly, are a closer
fit for these uses. They are not universally used, partly because no single
threshold clearly defines a clinically acceptable boundary error.

## What the benchmarks do not contain

Public brain tumour benchmarks consist overwhelmingly of high-grade glioma in
adults, imaged pre-operatively, on a narrow set of acquisition protocols. That
excludes postoperative patients with treatment-related change, lower-grade
tumours, non-neoplastic lesions, paediatric cases, and every scanner outside the
contributing institutions.

Excluded cases are not randomly distributed. They are the difficult cases, and
they are the ones on which a clinician would most want assistance. A model
whose published score is high will tend to degrade on exactly the population
that was never represented.

## Evaluating outside the benchmark

Three practices would improve the alignment between published evaluation and
clinical use:

1. **Report a boundary-sensitive metric alongside Dice.** If the downstream use
   is spatial, the primary metric should be spatial.
2. **Evaluate on a locally sourced cohort.** A held-out external set drawn from
   the institution that will deploy the model is worth more than any additional
   percentage point on a public benchmark.
3. **Report performance stratified by case difficulty,** including the failure
   cases. A single mean accuracy over a curated cohort conceals the distribution
   that matters.

## A practical note for new researchers

There is a real temptation to treat public benchmarks as a shortcut to
publication, and the temptation is understandable. The counter-argument is not
that this is improper, but that it is crowded: many groups can run the same
comparison, and a modest increment on a saturated benchmark is a weak
contribution. The same effort spent on an excluded population is likely to
support a stronger paper and a more useful model.
