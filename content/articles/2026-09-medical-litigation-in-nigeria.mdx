## Introduction

Medical litigation in Nigeria is undergoing a transformative shift. Historically, many patients viewed adverse medical outcomes as inevitable or beyond challenge.¹ However, a confluence of rising legal awareness, the impact of high-profile cases, and increased access to information has empowered patients to seek accountability through the courts.¹ This surge in litigation is not merely a legal phenomenon; it is a critical signal of a healthcare system under strain. As medical students and future practitioners, understanding the landscape of medical negligence, when clinical actions fall below the accepted standard of care, is essential. ¹,² Recognizing these trends is no longer optional; it is a fundamental requirement for practicing safe, ethical, and defensive medicine in a climate where accountability is increasingly prioritized.²

## Challenges and Clinical Practice

Current literature identifies that medical litigation in Nigeria arises from a complex interplay of individual clinical errors and systemic failures.¹ Common legal challenges include misdiagnosis, surgical errors, medication mistakes, and the critical failure to obtain informed consent.¹ Systemic pressures, such as the "Japa effect" leading to severe understaffing, chronic infrastructure deficits, and poor medical record-keeping, significantly exacerbate the risk of litigation.¹ Furthermore, unethical practices, such as the unlawful detention of patients over unpaid bills, are increasingly becoming grounds for successful legal action.¹

## Recommendations and Public Health Implications

**Systemic Investment:** Infrastructure deficits and staffing shortages are direct contributors to clinical errors.¹ Hospitals must prioritize investment in functional diagnostic equipment and support systems to reduce physician burnout.¹

**Institutionalizing ADR:** Given the time and cost burdens of traditional litigation, healthcare facilities should embrace Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) mechanisms, as encouraged by the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) Act 2022, to resolve patient grievances before they escalate to court.²

**Educational Integration:** Medical curricula must integrate practical training on medical law, ethics, and record-keeping.² Practitioners need to understand doctrines like Res Ipsa Loquitur (shift of burden of proof to the defendant) and the legal weight of their duties.²

**Culture of Transparency:** Moving toward a culture where near-misses are reported and discussed, without fear of immediate punitive action, can help identify systemic flaws before they manifest as patient harm.¹

Ultimately, protecting the patient through better practice is the most effective way to protect the provider from the courtroom.
