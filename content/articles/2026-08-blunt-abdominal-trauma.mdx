## Introduction

Blunt abdominal trauma (BAT) is a major cause of death and disability worldwide, and outcomes depend heavily on how quickly a patient gets care[4]. Heavy bleeding causes hypovolemic shock, and if not corrected, patients can enter the lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy, a cycle that worsens survival the longer it continues[5,6]. Unlike penetrating injury, blunt trauma often shows no outside signs, raising the risk of missed diagnosis[4].

This is a Nigerian problem right now. Rural BAT is mostly linked to commercial motorcycle and tricycle road crashes, while urban BAT is road traffic injuries[1,2]. Outside big cities, the fast transport, imaging, and blood banks golden hour teaching assumes are often missing. Most patients did not reach hospital within the golden hour, and very few reach the theatre within an hour of arrival[7]. This article looks at how BAT is managed outside Nigeria's big cities and proposes ways to improve outcomes.

## The Core

Nigerian data on BAT is limited and comes mostly from single hospitals rather than national records[3,9]. Even so, clear patterns emerge. In one southwestern Nigerian study, only a small number of patients reached hospital within the golden hour, and fewer  reached theatre within an hour of arrival[7]. In a southeastern Nigerian splenic injury study, more than one in six patients arrived more than 24 hours after injury, and nearly a third were already in shock[3]. In rural, conflict-affected southeastern Nigeria, fewer than one in four patients reached a specialist surgeon within six hours, and death rates were higher in patients who arrived late[9]. A 2024 national survey of Nigerian trauma surgeons found prehospital care minimal everywhere, worse outside cities, with untrained first responders, long transport times, and no national prehospital care standard[8]. This is a pattern that fits the wider sub-Saharan African picture; as rural-urban gaps in trauma care access are common and internal disparities are often underappreciated[13].

Diagnosis is also a challenge. Many facilities lack CT machines, so decisions rely on ultrasound, paracentesis, or diagnostic peritoneal lavage[7,10]. Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) is now the main diagnostic tool in low-resource Nigerian settings, but it has real limits for occult or retroperitoneal injury, so repeated clinical checks matter as much as any single test[3,10].

**Management is selective rather than fixed:** stability, number of injuries, and time since injury decide between surgery and conservative care[3]. Non-operative management can work even without ICU-level monitoring, as long as reassessment is rigorous[11]. But minimally invasive options like laparoscopy remain rare outside well-equipped centers[12].

## Recommendations / Public Health Implications

**Early Presentation and Community First Response:** Delayed presentation is directly linked to higher mortality in Nigerian BAT patients[9]. Getting patients to care fast is a joint responsibility: individuals and communities must seek care early, especially through primary healthcare, while government must fund transport dedicated to rural health emergencies. Brief community first-responder training in hemorrhage recognition and transport coordination supports this directly.

**Equip Primary Health Centres:** Primary health centres are the frontline in most rural communities, but many lack the basic equipment needed for resuscitation and stabilization. Adequate resourcing of these facilities would reduce preventable deaths before referral is even possible.

**Train Personnel in Structured Trauma Assessment:** Initial assessment of BAT should follow a structured approach such as the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ABCDE) framework, which prioritizes rapid evaluation and stabilization[16]. Proper diagnosis depends on trained personnel, so training existing staff and posting trained personnel to rural facilities should be a priority.

**Build An Uninterrupted Referral Chain:** Some BAT cases can be managed conservatively, but others need an advanced centre. A fast, reliable referral pathway from primary to secondary or tertiary care, with a clear stability-based decision point, reduces the complications that come from delay[3], though deciding whether to stabilize and transfer or operate locally remains a critical, evidence-poor decision in remote settings[14].

**Address The Funding Gap:** Cost is a major, often overlooked barrier: many rural Nigerian families face catastrophic health costs paying for BAT care, and this financial delay is itself linked to worse outcomes[15]. Any pathway proposed here needs a way to pay for care, not just a way to reach it. Realistic near-term options include a state-level emergency trauma fund covering surgery costs up front, and partnerships with NGOs already working in rural Nigerian health.
