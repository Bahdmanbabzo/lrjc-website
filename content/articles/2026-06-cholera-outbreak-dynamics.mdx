## Introduction

Cholera is an acute diarrheal disease caused by Vibrio cholerae serogroups O1 and O139. It is transmitted mainly through contaminated water and food and can lead to severe dehydration and death if prompt treatment is not provided. Despite being a preventable and treatable disease, cholera remains a major public health problem in Nigeria due to poor sanitation, inadequate access to clean water, poverty, and weaknesses in the healthcare system. The country experiences recurrent outbreaks, especially during the rainy season, with significant impacts on morbidity, mortality, and healthcare resources. Understanding the pattern of cholera outbreaks and evaluating Nigeria's health system response are essential for improving prevention, preparedness, and disease control strategies (1–3)

## Outbreak Dynamics of Cholera in Nigeria

Cholera remains one of the major endemic infectious diseases in Nigeria and continues to pose a significant public health challenge. The disease spreads primarily through contaminated water and food, making communities with poor access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) particularly vulnerable. Since the 1970s, Nigeria has experienced repeated cholera outbreaks, resulting in thousands of illnesses and deaths. One of the largest outbreaks occurred in 1991, when over 59,000 cases and more than 7,600 deaths were reported, with a case fatality rate (CFR) of 12.9%, far above the World Health Organization's recommended target of less than 1%.

Since then, Nigeria has continued to experience periodic outbreaks of varying magnitude, across successive outbreak seasons. Although the CFR declined to approximately 3.7% by 2024, indicating improvements in case detection, clinical management, and outbreak response, cholera remains a persistent health threat (1,2,4)

## Health System Responses to Cholera in Nigeria

Nigeria's response to cholera is coordinated by the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) through a comprehensive approach that includes disease surveillance, prompt case management, vaccination, water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) interventions, and community engagement. Surveillance systems enable the early detection and reporting of outbreaks, allowing rapid response teams to intervene promptly. During outbreaks, emergency coordination centres improve collaboration among government agencies and partners, ensuring efficient case management and distribution of oral cholera vaccines to high-risk communities. Preventive efforts focus on improving access to safe water, promoting sanitation and hygiene practices, distributing water treatment supplies, and educating communities through public awareness campaigns in English and local languages to encourage early reporting and adoption of preventive measures (2,5)

## Challenges of Cholera Outbreak Control in Nigeria

Despite ongoing efforts to control cholera, Nigeria continues to experience recurrent outbreaks driven by several interconnected challenges. Limited access to safe drinking water, poor sanitation, and inadequate hygiene practices remain the primary factors sustaining the transmission of Vibrio cholerae, particularly in rural communities and densely populated urban settlements (1,6)

Seasonal flooding further worsens the situation by contaminating water sources, disrupting sanitation systems, and displacing populations, thereby increasing the risk of disease transmission. In addition, weaknesses in disease surveillance, delayed reporting of suspected cases, inadequate laboratory capacity, and shortages of trained healthcare personnel and essential medical supplies can delay outbreak detection and effective case management (2,5)

Socioeconomic and community-related factors also play a significant role. Poverty, poor health-seeking behaviour, low public awareness of cholera prevention, and the spread of misinformation often reduce adherence to preventive measures and delay access to appropriate healthcare. Addressing these challenges requires sustained investment in water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, strengthened surveillance systems, improved healthcare capacity, and continuous community health education to reduce the burden of cholera in Nigeria (1,5,6)

## Recommendations

Continuous community health education should be prioritized to promote hand hygiene, safe water use, proper sanitation, and early healthcare seeking for acute diarrheal illness. Governments should invest in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure to reduce cholera transmission. Disease surveillance, laboratory capacity, and rapid response systems should be strengthened to ensure early detection and effective outbreak control (7,8). Oral cholera vaccines should be deployed in high-risk communities as part of an integrated prevention strategy alongside WASH interventions. Health facilities should also be adequately equipped with essential medicines, diagnostic tools, oral rehydration salts, intravenous fluids, and trained healthcare workers to improve case management and reduce cholera-related morbidity and mortality (4,5,8)

## Conclusion

Cholera remains a significant public health challenge in Nigeria, driven by inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene, weak surveillance systems, and limited access to quality healthcare. Although progress has been made through improved surveillance, oral cholera vaccination, and coordinated outbreak response, recurrent epidemics highlight the need for sustained preventive measures. Strengthening WASH infrastructure, enhancing community awareness, improving health system preparedness, and fostering multisectoral collaboration are essential for reducing the burden of cholera. A comprehensive and coordinated approach will be critical to preventing future outbreaks and improving health outcomes in Nigeria.
