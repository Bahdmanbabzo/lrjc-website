## Introduction

Healthcare content creation has increased significantly over the past few years, with social media platforms providing healthcare professionals and students opportunities to educate the public, raise awareness about diseases, and combat health misinformation.[1,2] Beyond educational content, many healthcare workers now share daily hospital vlogs, behind-the-scenes experiences, and other forms of digital content.[1] In Nigeria, healthcare professionals are increasingly using social media for health communication and public engagement. While these forms of content can improve public health literacy and make healthcare more relatable, they may also cross important legal and ethical boundaries.[1] Recording activities during ward rounds or patient interactions can compromise patient confidentiality, informed consent, dignity, and professional conduct.[1,3,4]As social media continues to influence healthcare communication in Nigeria, there is an urgent need to balance the benefits of digital engagement with the ethical and legal responsibilities that underpin safe and professional medical practice.[1,3]

## The Core Issues of Healthcare Content Creation

Arguably, social media has been a tremendously helpful tool for facilitating health awareness, enlightening the public about preventive measures, and demystifying life-threatening diseases. Despite these advantages, the digital space poses critical legal and ethical dangers to healthcare delivery in Nigeria:

### 1) Breach of Confidentiality

Section 26 of the National Health Act (2014) obligates medical professionals to maintain the absolute privacy of patient records. Clinical information must never be shared without explicit patient authorization. Because clinical relationships rely entirely on privacy, broadcasting patient details or case discussions from ward rounds on TikTok erodes public trust in the healthcare system and severely decreases vital health-seeking behavior.[5]

### 2) Destruction of Professional Image

Some clinicians utilize social media to aggressively expand their client base. This conflicts with Part F of the Code of Medical Ethics in Nigeria regarding self-advertisement and media relationships, which restricts public advertising to safeguard professional decorum. Furthermore, mixing clinical authority with viral video entertainment means a single misjudged trend or unprofessional post can instantly tarnish a practitioner’s or an institution’s reputation.[6,7]

### 3) Authenticity of Information

Social media has alarmingly become the default medical consultant for the general public due to massive information overload. The explosion of unverified 'medfluencers' and digital subcultures like 'DiabetesTok' has created a dangerous digital 'infodemic.'[8] This open landscape allows bad actors to hide behind pseudo-medical profiles, spreading inaccurate and unverified information while exploiting the innocence of vulnerable populations who rely on them for medical advice.[8]

## Recommendations

Navigating the intersection of medical practice and digital content creation requires a proactive, grounded approach tailored to the unique realities of Nigerian clinical environments. To avoid the significant legal and ethical pitfalls that come with recording ward rounds, Nigerian clinicians and medical students must put patient privacy above all else. Under current national data regulations and the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria guidelines, sharing patient narratives, clinical photographs, or even video clips where a hospital ward layout can be recognized is unacceptable without explicit, documented consent.[9,10] It is easy to forget that even well-intentioned “day in the life of a doctor” vlogs can lead to unintended privacy leaks through simple circumstantial details, which can quickly pull down institutional trust and compromise patient dignity.[9]

At the same time, maintaining professional boundaries online is non-negotiable. Healthcare workers should actively separate their personal social media profiles from their professional personas, making sure they do not inadvertently create informal doctor-patient relationships via direct messages or comments sections.[11] To address this structurally, Nigerian teaching hospitals and colleges of medicine need to step up by establishing clear, local social media policies and embedding digital jurisprudence into undergraduate clinical orientations.[12] From a broader public health perspective, platforms like TikTok offer an incredible opportunity to challenge health myths and educate communities, but this must be done with restraint and respect.[1] Only distribute evidence-based healthcare information, correct any errors, and properly attribute shared content.[13]  While social media is a powerful vehicle for health advocacy, virtual behavior ultimately carries the exact same professional liability, disciplinary weight, and legal risks as actions taken at a physical bedside.[11,12]
