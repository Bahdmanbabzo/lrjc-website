## Introduction

Cerebrospinal meningitis is a severe disease characterized by inflammation of the protective tissues (meninges) surrounding the brain and spinal cord. It is commonly caused by infectious agents, particularly Neisseria meningitidis.¹ It is a significant public health concern in Sub-Saharan Africa, with the highest burden occurring in the African Meningitis Belt, a region stretching from Senegal to Ethiopia and including Nigeria.¹˒² Meningitis outbreaks in this region show a strong seasonal pattern, with incidence rising to as high as 100 cases per 100,000 population during the dry season.²˒³ Environmental factors such as climate, dust winds, cold nights, and upper respiratory tract infections damage the nasopharyngeal mucosa, increasing vulnerability to meningitis and facilitating transmission.² For Nigerian medical students, understanding the relationship between seasonality and meningitis is crucial, as future clinicians and public health practitioners must be equipped to recognize outbreak patterns, support preventive measures, and respond effectively to epidemics.¹˒³

## Environmental Factors Facilitating Meningitis Transmission in the African Belt

The dry season has contributed indirectly to the rise in cases of meningitis seen in the African meningitis belt 4. During this season, certain factors like the harmattan winds, low relative humidity and mineral dust concentrations are at their peak 5. It is associated with the rise in the cases of meningitis and is seen to be gone with the beginning of rainfall 8. The upper respiratory tract (nose, throat, and nasopharynx), which is normally lined by mucus, is the target of the Neisseria meningitidis bacteria 5. They get exposed to the dust and dry air 5. The dry air causes the mucus to lose its moisture, and the increased inhalation of dust can irritate this tract 5,6,9. Dryness and irritation break down the mucosal defenses, allowing for Neisseria Meningitidis to adhere easily to the respiratory tract, multiply and enter the bloodstream to cause meningitis. 5

## The Human Dimension of Seasonality: Behavior, Crowding, and Meningitis Transmission

Seasonality shapes meningococcal meningitis not only through environmental conditions but also by infuencing human behavior. During the dry season in the African meningitis belt, cold nighttime temperatures and dusty Harmattan winds encourage people to spend more time indoors, often in overcrowded and poorly ventilated homes, classrooms, and places of worship. These conditions increase close interpersonal contact, facilitating the transmission of Neisseria meningitidis through respiratory droplets. Evidence suggests that while meningococcal carriage may not increase substantially during the dry season, seasonal environmental stress and behavioral factors increase the likelihood that asymptomatic carriage progresses to invasive disease. 10,11

Studies have also shown that household crowding, close sleeping proximity, and exposure to indoor air pollution from cooking fres signifcantly increase the risk of meningococcal carriage and transmission 12. Adolescents and young adults, who typically have the highest social contact rates, play an important role in sustaining subclinical carriage and community transmission. 13 Furthermore, investigations conducted by the MenAfriCar Consortium demonstrated that transmission occurs both within households and through wider community interactions, emphasizing the importance of social mixing patterns in disease spread. 14

These fndings highlight that efective meningitis prevention extends beyond vaccination. Public health interventions should also promote improved indoor ventilation, reduce overcrowding where feasible, minimize exposure to indoor smoke, and strengthen community awareness during the high-risk dry season. Integrating these anthropological and behavioral considerations into meningitis control strategies is essential for reducing transmission and preventing seasonal epidemics across the African meningitis belt. 11,14

## Recommendations

1. We need the government, at every level, to adequately prepare all concerned health facilities at the very instant a meningitis outbreak is forecasted, and to make special preparations for children and the elderly 15,16. Meningitis must be combated with systems that prevent it from thriving in its favourable climate 16. The International Coordinating Group should be called upon in times of need 15. Meningitis outbreak can be predicted and the predictions should be met with proper preventive measures 15.
2. Actions should be taken to extend the coverage of the meningitis prediction model. Governmental and non-governmental organisations should work together to see that the model’s coverage extend to areas such as the Congo Basin and the Namibian desert, to further help in meningitis prevention 15.
3. Several insights can still be garnered from Africa’s climatic conditions. These insights can help us in further reducing the occurrence of other climatic-dependent diseases. And this holds for meningitis also, as there are even more critical analyses that can be done on the existing data 17.
4. Direct, rapid medical intervention to stop an active or rising outbreak in its tracks by building immediate herd immunity, cerebrospinal meningitis is highly contagious and spreads through respiratory droplets. Temporarily restricting crowded social events and the movement of known carriers acts as a physical circuit breaker to halt transmission during peak outbreak seasons.18
